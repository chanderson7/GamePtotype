# Bannerfall

*Working title.* Real-time squad battles sized for a phone, inside a sandbox campaign in the spirit of Mount & Blade, set in an invented world. Built with **Godot 4.7** in GDScript, targeting iPhone first, with Steam and Android from the same project.

This slice is the start of a campaign: pick a people, ride the map of the Glass Sea, and fight the bandits on its roads in real-time battles, or send your men in without you. Your warband carries over between battles and has to be paid and fed: the men you lose stay lost until you pay to reinforce them in a town, bandit bounties and spoils keep the purse full, squads that kill can be trained, and renown lets you lead more squads.

## What's in it

### The campaign

- **Lords and bandits you can read on the map.** Each realm's lords ride their own horse (a bay for Vardmark, a sand horse for Kharzan, a near-black one for Ryumon) under the realm's cloth and banner, with a knight's great helm and lance, an emir's turban and curved blade, or a Ryumon lord's crested helmet and back-flag; their men march behind, one per 45 men up to two, so a host's size is counted, not guessed. Bandits look like their region's outlaws (hooded brigands, masked desert reavers, ronin in straw hats, Wako pirates), sit round a fire while they lie in wait, and carry torches under smoke while they raid. Name plates are edged red for enemies, brass for friends, and carry a chip with the men and how they compare with yours (▲ stronger, ◆ about even, ▼ weaker).
- **Realm banners.** Each realm flies its own banner, with its own shape so you can tell them apart at a glance: Vardmark a pointed gonfalon with a silver keep on blue, Kharzan a swallowtail with a crimson sun over teal waters on saffron, Ryumon a tall nobori with three crossed arrows on red; your own Free Crown a gold crown on night, and bandits a torn rag with crossed knives. They fly over castles and towns and with parties on the map, in settlement views, over a besieged keep and on the siege result, on Home and on the new-campaign cards.
- **An opening of its own.** No Godot logo: the boot splash is night on a ridge, the three realms' banners dark against the stars, and *Bannerfall* under them. When the game has loaded, that same picture comes to life over Home: dawn rises behind the ridge in stepped bands, the banners light one by one and sway, a glint runs across the title, then the land drops away and the title rises into its place on Home as the night lifts. About three seconds, once a launch; a tap hurries it on, and Reduced motion leaves it out. The splash (`assets/splash.png`) is a render of the opening's first frame, covering the screen the same way on any shape of phone or tablet (and on the iOS launch screen), so the hand-over has no jump; re-render it with `tools/render_splash.gd` (its header has the command) if the opening changes.
- **A Home screen for your company.** Your company on one card over the drifting map: the camp with your banner and title, where and when you left off, men, silver, food and renown as tiles, your captains' faces, and *Waiting for you* (unread letters, your contract, the wounded, the next step), each a tap from where it leads, then Continue. New campaign (with an explicit replacement confirmation), Learn to play and Settings sit in a small row below. The first time, Home shows the three peoples to begin with. Settings has its own page, reachable from Home or the map's Menu; Warband stays focused on your company.
- **Optional tutorial.** The first time Home opens, *Learn to play* stands above the three peoples; and the first campaign started on a device has the tutorial switched on (off once a campaign has begun there or the lessons were opened). Replay the six-step introduction from Home or Settings. Go back, skip, or leave at any point without advancing the campaign. On the new-campaign page the tutorial switch comes first; whether captains can die (Mortal or Casual) and Ironman wait folded under *More options*.
- **The next step.** Home and the map's Menu carry one piece of advice from where the campaign stands: before the first fight, the nearest bandit band by name (*Show on map* opens it); with too little silver for tomorrow's food or wages, a contract at the nearest town or castle's notice board (or the weakest band near, when no board has work); *Room for another company* only when the hire leaves three days of wages; for a vassal, the renown a crown needs and whether a castle stands free to ask for from the village's Fief tab; for a crown, how many of the castles and towns it holds.
- **Resources and warnings.** Silver, food and renown have labeled icons in the map's top-right corner. Tap one for details; food is shown in days. Low supplies warn that men may leave. If they do, a saved warning names the losses and causes, explains what to fix, and pauses time until acknowledged.
- **Your first bond.** When a surviving captain pair first reaches rank C, a one-time introduction explains morale recovery, nearby deployment, and the later B and A bonuses. Open Warband directly to review the pair; the acknowledgment is saved with the campaign. After that, bonds never stop you: a battle's results tell them once (ranks reached, then "Fenwick remembers Brann" for bonds a death ended; pairs that only grew a little closer go unsaid), and each new rank's talk waits at the fire.
- **A UI that moves like a game.** Every button sinks under the thumb and springs back, and brass ones tick; sheets ease out the way they ease in; a tab change slides its underline and turns the page; what a tap cost or brought rises off the silver or food, a lord's new Regard rises by his face, and what a tap made glows; the battle result tells itself in under two seconds (tap to finish); hours that passed out of sight sweep by on the map's clock; a road encounter brings the camera in on the two parties; letters drop in from behind the top bar; deeds, captains ready for a role, a band riding after you and a war declared arrive as a ribbon (held longer at bigger text; a tap puts it away); their words in a conversation are written out (Settings: *Text appears at once*); faces slide into a scene, rooms after dark breathe with firelight, and the wedding's bunting sways. All of it is still under *Reduced motion*.
- **Battle results that say only what happened.** The result card is the picture of the field with its place, why it ended, the tallies as tiles (your slain and wounded, theirs down) and the spoils counting up. Under *Your companies* are only the companies with something to tell, fallen or wounded captains first: the captain's face with a badge for their fate (a star for the hardest fighting), men out of men in, kills, the death roll and *Ready to train* when this fight earned it. The rest are one line, "8 other companies saw no fighting."

- **The Glass Sea.** An invented inland sea shared by three realms: the **Kingdom of Vardmark** on the northwest shore (stone keeps and armored knights), the **Sultanate of Kharzan** across the southern deserts (walled cities and swift riders), and the **Ryumon Shogunate** on the islands beyond the eastern strait (disciplined archers and spearmen). Twenty-one settlements: in each realm a town, two castles and four villages, every village bound to a castle or town, joined by roads and three sea lanes. Who holds each place, and who is at war with whom, are the world's own and kept in your save, ready for the realms to fight over them.
- **Choose your people.** A new campaign starts with a choice of culture. Your captains take its names, your companies its words ("Brann's Pikes", "Tariq's Spears", "Hideo's Yari"), your troops its names (Footmen, Askari, Samurai), and you start at its capital. Cultures fight alike for now; their stats will differ later.
- **Ride the roads.** Tap a settlement to see it and ride there. Time passes only while you ride or wait, an hour a second (two at 2x), and stops the moment anything needs you. Sea lanes are slower than roads. Drag to look around, pinch or scroll to zoom, double-tap to find your party.
- **A living map.** A ragged coastline with beaches and surf breathing against it, turquoise shallows over a deep sea, hills shaded by the light, and each realm's own country: Vardmark's meadows, woods and grey peaks, Kharzan's sand, dunes, palms and red mesas, the Ryumon isles' pines, cherry blossom and snow-capped mountains, with fields, dry plots or paddies around every village. Three rivers run down to the sea: the Raven past Ravensford, the Qamar through the desert, and the Kumo on the isles, each forded where a road crosses it. Each culture builds its own towns, castles and villages: Vardish stone keeps under slate and red tile, Kharzani sandstone walls, domes and minarets, Ryumon castles of stacked black roofs on stone bases. Your rider trots along the road under your banner, kicking up dust; bandit bands march under ragged ones. The light follows the clock, gold at dawn and dusk and blue at night, when the settlements' lamps are lit, and cloud shadows drift across land and sea. A campaign starts at eight in the morning.
- **Lords on the map.** Each realm has its ruler and four lords, two of them holding its castles, riding the roads with warbands of five to nine squads under captains of their people, more of them trained as their renown grows. Each has a bearing that steers him (*Daring*, *Cautious*, *Honorable*, *Cruel*). They keep watch on their lands, drive off bandits caught raiding their villages, ride to defend a village the enemy is burning, raid the villages of realms they're at war with (a village left burning pays nothing and offers no recruits for a week), and go home to refill when their warbands are spent. Lords of realms at war who meet fight it out, settled as a fight you send your men into would be, and the loser regroups at his seat. A realm's treasury takes in what its places pay and pays its lords. Tap a lord to see who they are (with a portrait and their house's banner), how strong, and what they're about; zoom in to see their names. Lords at war with you stop you on the road; friendly ones stop to talk while you ride. Each speaks in the manner of their bearing, and one who has fought you before remembers it.
- **Talking to lords.** Meet a lord and you talk face to face, where you met: before a painted place (Ravensford, Qasr Hamra, Kitayama, your camp) or the settlement as the game paints it, at the hour it is, with their portrait, their banner and yours. One exchange at a time: they speak, and you answer with a line tagged with what it does, nothing irreversible on a stray tap. Friendly lords stop you when they have something to say; tap one nearby (or a lord of a realm at peace with you) to *Talk*, and a lord blocking your road can be talked to before steel is drawn. A lord you've talked with before greets you as someone they know, and the head of a house you married into (or swore a pact with) greets you as kin (or a sworn friend); People → Lords marks them *Kin by marriage* or *Sworn pact*. Every greeting, challenge and battle's end has two lines for each bearing, taken in turn.
  - *Regard*, -100 to 100, is what a lord thinks of you: what you've done to and for them, on top of their realm's opinion and your fame. It shows on their map sheet, in the talk and in People.
  - *News*: what's happened in the war since a lord last told you, and what they think of their own ruler.
  - *Stories*: tell one from your chronicle or the company's tales, each to each lord once. Cautious lords like prudence, Daring ones boldness, Honorable ones modesty and Cruel ones a hard hand; the game remembers what you learn.
  - *Troubles*: brigands on their lands become a job in your journal (haggle for silver, if they don't mind haggling). Done, it's worth +15 Regard, the biggest single source.
  - *Winning a lord over*, once you've raised your own banner: their conviction fills toward 100 from their Regard, jobs done for them, their ruler's failings once they've told you of them, arguments they like and silver. At 100 they swear on the spot, and a landed lord brings their castle and its villages. Push arguments they dislike and they refuse you (-10 Regard and a week's wait). A lord won mostly with silver is *bought*, and goes back, castle and all, if they come to think badly of you. Rulers never change sides.
  - *Steel*: challenge a lord to battle (at peace, that means war) with the stakes shown first, or to single combat with one of your captains as champion (no armies, no war: win for Regard and renown, lose and your champion is wounded for two battles). A Cruel lord blocking your road takes tribute.
  - *Captives* are talked to at your camp: swear them to your crown (Honorable ones won't), release them for honor (+25 Regard, +10 with their realm the first time you free that lord; half that, and no renown, after), ransom them (their house pays what its treasury and their purse hold), or keep them in chains, which Honorable lords everywhere hear of.
  - *People → Lords* lists every lord you've met: their Regard, what you know of them, whether they'd swear, and a pin to find them on the map.
- **Bandits.** Two bands roam the villages of each realm. When one blocks your road you can lead the fight, in a full battle against an army led by captains of that realm; send your men in without you, settled on the spot at the cost of a few more men; or withdraw to the last settlement you passed. Your scouts size up the fight first: who's stronger, and about how many men you'd lose sending them in; when they call it Hard or Deadly, Send in asks for a second tap. A band always lets you withdraw; only a lord who is quicker and far stronger may ride you down, and the sheet says so before you choose: it reads *Can't withdraw* and offers no Withdraw. A beaten band is gone; another rises in its realm a day later. If they beat you, your survivors regroup at your capital half a day later, and the band keeps only the men it had left.
- **Garrisons.** Castles and towns keep guards of their local culture: up to 80 men in a castle, with more trained troops, and 120 in a town. They start half staffed and recruit a few men each day from their owner's treasury after the lords are paid, while keeping a reserve for wages. Settlement descriptions show the current guards and capacity; losses and empty garrisons persist in your save.
- **Romance and allies.** Meet three women attached to noble houses: Edda, the Vardish steward at Ravensford; Farah, the Kharzani envoy at Qasr Hamra; and Kaede, the Ryumon archivist at Kitayama. Each has a distinct five-chapter route and six optional illustrated personal conversations, with honest and strained branches, repair restitution, public weddings, private vows, or sworn friendships. Form house pacts for mutual protection, emergency household grants (up to 150 silver from a solvent lord), and replacement sponsorship escrow to fund allied recruits. In People & Letters each person has a card (portrait matching their mood, status, trust, the five chapters of their story, the next step and its distance, and one button to ride there or speak with them) and a page of their own (their story chapter by chapter, what they care about and won't stand for, dated memories). Their letters are real letters: written for them and for whether you're courting or friends, on parchment with the place and day, a signature and their house seal. Answer each with a short reply you read before it goes (a warm one warms them a little; each chapter's letter has answers of its own for each of them). New letters arrive as a banner on the map that never stops the clock, and the menu button counts what's unread. Only someone you've met writes, and when your own company took her home, her letter says so instead of consoling you. Say nothing at the first audience ("We'll speak another time") and you've still met: she asks again what you mean to be to each other at any later chapter. Standing by her in council costs her house's goodwill, so from the council on you can pay for a muster at her house's seat (100 silver, every three days, up to +25 standing); the locked wedding or pact says so. Married or sworn, she writes when you win a battle near her, when a captain falls and when you've been away twenty days, an evening together is always there to be had, and a day after the vows one more matter waits as a sixth chapter. Edda signs with the name of the house that really holds Ravensford, and when a lord dies his heir heads his house by name. Toggle romance invitations in Settings.
- **Talking with them.** Speak with someone and you're face to face in their room (Edda's common room, Farah's envoy's chamber, Kaede's archive), at the hour it is, their painted portrait changing with what they say, their status and trust on the nameplate once there is any (the bar is marked at 60, where vows and a wedding open; before you've met, only "Not yet met"). The visit is a conversation: their greeting, then a few things you might say in your own words, each with what it is under it ("I came about Thornwall." · *Chapter 3 · An Obligation Shared*; "Nothing that needs doing today. I just came." · *time together · The Spare Spoon*; "Your letter found me on the road."; "Remember when…"), and one way to take your leave. The story's choices are things you'd say too, with the task, a cost or a change in how things stand under them (*into the district's fund · 300 silver*; *Edda's trust · the elders' displeasure*; a broken word in red) and never a score; what a choice did is told afterwards as how they take it (*Edda trusts you more*, *Edda's house takes note*) while the trust bar moves, and only the step that asks you to be sure (a wedding, private vows, parting, breaking your word) shows the numbers. Their lines sit in their bubble, narration between, yours on the right; a visit is read the same way, with the stage lowered to make room, tap or *Go on* to continue, *Short version* and *Another time* beside it. A kept visit ends with a keepsake: "A memory kept…", and "+1 trust (2 of 3)" only when trust moved. The memory book keeps each story choice as a line of its own ("The day the company held the valley."), and *Remember when…* has them answer with what they remember of it. The story keeps a pace: each chapter opens a day after the last decision, and her letter comes with it (an invitation for every chapter, in her voice, for a courtship or a friendship); until then the chapter line in the talk is locked with "tomorrow; she'll write", and the People page says so. One new visit a day ("It's late. Another day."; a visit begun earlier can always be picked up), and the last chapter waits until she trusts you enough to be asked anything (50). And she knows what has happened since you last came: her first words go to a captain you lost ("Word of Brann reached us before you did"), a wounded one, a company that limped in, an army under her own walls or ash by her door, a war between your realms, a battle you won within two days' ride, twenty days away, or the lateness of the hour, each in her own voice. A war, a siege or ashes open one visit, not every one, and a wound only when it's new since you last came; with nothing new, one of two greetings for how things stand, in turn. Chapter 3's task (holding her roads, carrying her letters) goes in your journal as a job bound to her seat; the chapter ends when the job does, and she tells you how it went at your next visit. Parted, she won't start a new visit, and says why in her own words; what you shared can still be told again. *Remember when…* pages back through every memory, and she answers vows by one candle, each dance and the council in words of her own.
- **The wedding.** Ask before her house and she says yes, then asks where: her house's seat, your own seat if you hold one, or another friendly town near you (100 silver and 100 rations are set aside, and come back if you call it off). The place is made ready by the first dusk a day later, she writes when it is, and a gold ring marks it on the map; it waits for you, with no deadline. A siege, ashes or a realm at war with you holding the place postpones it, and her letter asks you to choose again. Ride there and *Begin your wedding*: the place is dressed in pixel bunting, both banners and lanterns; *Who came* shows her house's head as host, lords who think well of you, your sworn lords if you wear a crown and all your captains, and says who stayed away and why. Her vow comes from a time you shared ("She remembers: the spare spoon"); yours is one of three, none wrong. Then her people's rite (the Vardish cord, Kharzani bread and salt, Ryumon's three cups), a toast from your longest-serving captain and one from the host, and you raise your cup (to her, to the company, or to absent friends, by name, if captains have fallen). One closing card shows the kept memory with your words, the marriage pact in plain words (it protects her seat and yours), Regard from the lords who came (+10 host, +5 each other), and +15 renown. Private vows happen right in her room by one candle, with the same vows, no guests and no cost, and say plainly that her house wasn't asked. Her page then reads "Married · since day N", and the wedding in her memory book tells itself again with your own vow and toast.
- **The hall and gatherings.** Every town and castle has a Court tab ("Court · 3") listing who is really there: the lord who holds it if they're home, lords resting on their way, the household. Each row has their face, what they're doing ("Holds Ravensford · at home", "Visiting · resting before the road"), their Regard, and a brass "would like a word" when they have a trouble or a visit is open. *Away* says where the holder has gone, with *Show on map*. Tap a lord to talk indoors, in their culture's hall drawn in pixel art (a Vardish great hall with its dais and hearth, a Kharzani courtyard of arches and lanterns, a Ryumon audience hall of paper screens and tatami), tinted for the hour, with the holder's banners. No swords under a host's roof: a challenge becomes "Meet me outside the walls at dawn", a duel. In peace each realm holds a gathering about every two weeks, and a peace treaty brings a peace feast at the capital (and taking a castle, a victory feast there once it's safe): the ruler at the capital or a rich lord at their seat, three nights from the next dusk, a feast or a dance by its culture's name (feast or midsummer dance, banquet or lantern night, moon-viewing or blossom festival). Up to eight lords within two days' ride come, unless they're on campaign, raiding, under orders or refilling; their plates read "riding to the peace feast", and the hall glows with a ring of lanterns on the map. You're invited if you serve the realm, hold land in it, rule an ally, or the host's Regard is 10 or more: it comes as a banner like a letter, and sits in the journal with *Who's coming* and *Ride there*. During it the tab is *Feast · 6* in brass: your first word with each lord at the table is +2 Regard (+1 uninvited), and you can raise a toast once (renown 120: +2 with everyone there), bring the host a gift (50 silver or rations: host +5, realm +2), and hear the gossip once a night (an hour; it only says true things: a lord sour with their ruler because their castle burned or was lost, a lord's trouble, the latest war news). A dance night (every night of a dance, the last of a feast) offers one partner, and never a lord himself: a warlord is for the table. Someone with a story who is in the hall (she lives there, or her house's lord is the host or a guest; Farah, an envoy, comes to every gathering of her realm): someone you've met (a dance, +1 trust up to three dances, kept as a memory) or someone you've never met, pointed out across the hall ("Ask the woman in Ravensford grey to dance"): a first word, kept as the night you met, and then the story's own first question, court her, as a friend or nothing decided, the same as at her first audience, which still asks it if you put it off. A lord's kinswoman, his wife, sister or daughter, named once and the same woman every time, the host's first and up to three (+3 Regard with her lord, +4 if he's Daring). The round everyone joins (+1 Regard with every lord in the hall). Or the wall and its gossip. Someone you court who isn't there is noted, with where she would be. War, a siege or an enemy army near breaks it up, and the news says why. With a crown, your seat's Fief tab holds your own (feast or dance, 300 silver and 200 rations, once every 20 days, at peace): sworn lords always come, and you choose whether to ask friends, lords you'd win over, and their lieges (leave a liege out and it's a small snub); each guest is +5 Regard once, and lords you're persuading arrive 10 points more convinced. Between gatherings, lords near their capital sometimes rest there a while, so halls are rarely empty.
- **Memories you can open.** Each person's page keeps a memory book: every visit, decision, letter, ceremony, dance and move, with its day, place and an icon. A visit tells itself again (as a memory: it changes nothing), a letter opens, a decision shows what you said and how they answered, a move shows the place on the map. A settlement's Court says "Edda would like a word · new" when there's something new; nothing starts on its own.
- **Silver, food and wages.** You start with 400 silver and five days of food. Every day costs each man a ration and his wages (more for archers, more again for horse, half again for each level of training). Men who go unpaid or hungry desert, about one in ten a day, fewer if you're only a little short, and the map tells you when it happens, and that a town's notice board pays without a victory. The top bar shows your men, your silver, how many days of food you have ("5d") and your renown, in red when you're running short, with *Food short* or *Can't pay* beside the status line when tomorrow can't be covered. Play and 2× while you stand still say why nothing moves: time passes while you ride or wait.
- **Spoils and renown.** Beating a band pays a bounty plus silver and food for every man cut down, and wins renown. You can lead six squads at first, and one more for every 60 renown, up to twelve.
- **Culture troop trees and training.** Training a squad to level 2 in any town presents a two-card choice between two distinct branches for that troop type and culture (e.g., Vardish Men-at-arms vs Sergeants, Knights vs Mounted Sergeants; Kharzani Javelin Askari vs Guard Askari, Horse Archers vs Faris Lancers; Ryumon Hatamoto vs Ronin, Naginata vs Yari Ashigaru). Level 3 strengthens the chosen branch. Six specialized mechanics: armor and piercing, pre-contact javelins, horse archer skirmishing and saddle-shooting, melee sweeps hitting secondary foes, and steady morale.
- **Varied encounter objectives.** Not every battle is an open field rout:
  - *Slay the Bandit Chief*: Led by a named chieftain at the head of a tier-2 bodyguard squad, which waits behind his line until the fight turns. Felling or routing the bodyguard drops the entire host's morale by 35. Break the guard while your men are on it and the chief goes down in the rout, with no one to drag him clear, and the death roll decides his fate: slain, for double bounty and +10 renown, or carried off by his men after all. A chief who gets away comes back five days later at the head of a new band.
  - *Ambush*: Caught on a wooded road, your warband is strung out in a column three abreast, unbraced, with the enemy coming at both ends and no time to form up. You cannot withdraw. Strike back at once, before they close in.
  - *Village Defense*: Arriving at a village being raided by bandits triggers a timed defense: the village lies behind your line, and you hold its square for 2 minutes while more raiders come at it in waves from east and west. If they hold the square two to one for 25 seconds in all, they fire the village and the fight is lost; a ring of flame round the square shows how near they are.
- **Battlefield terrain.** The ground comes from where the fight happens on the world map: hills in high country, woods where the map is wooded, and a river with its fords wherever the fight is near one of the map's rivers:
  - *Hills & Ridges*: Vantage grants archers up to +25% range. Blows struck down a slope land up to 40% harder, and up one as much softer; uphill charges yield no bonus. A ridge is a line of knolls and saddles, its brow swelling and falling along its length.
  - *Woods & Forests*: Slows foot by 25% and horse by 50%. Treetops deflect 50% of incoming arrows, halve volley shock, and prevent charges; blows struck into the trees from outside land 20% softer.
  - *River Fords*: Impassable deep water forces armies through shallow fords where troops are slowed, take 25% more damage, deal 20% less, and cannot brace.
  - *Rocky Passes*: Natural bottlenecks that block movement while letting arrows arc over: a mass of rock comes in from each side, a main outcrop astride the middle with a shoulder above and below, so the way through winds between them.
  - *Village Streets*: Buildings and walls channel combat into tight streets and defense perimeters.
- **The ground drawn as ground.** Each site's features are baked once when the battle starts (`scripts/battle/field_art.gd`), in the land's own colours: slopes lit from the upper left with dry grass on the brow and contour lines; a forest floor with litter, deadfall and the trees' shadows; a river with muddy, sandy or pebbled banks, reeds, a foam line, gravel fords and glinting water; outcrops of granite, sandstone or basalt as boulders with scree at their feet; a village's houses roofed as its people roof them (thatch and a chimney, flat mud brick and a dome, dark tile), round a trodden square with a well. Boulders stand on crests, bushes at the edge of a wood and along the banks, and the open ground carries soft patches of lighter and darker growth. None of it moves a man or slows him.
- **Chosen ground.** A band met on hills or in woods has sometimes picked its ground first (three times in ten when it isn't raiding or ambushing): it waits for you on the brow of a ridge or at the edge of a wood, and your scouts tell you so. Attacking it there costs you; going round or drawing it off is up to you.
- **Settlements.** Enter any settlement to buy food (cheapest in villages) and hire the recruits looking for work: a full squad of fresh men of that place's own culture under a captain of their own, so a Vardish warband can field Ryumon archers. Villages raise foot and bows, castles spears and horse, towns anything; recruits taken come back three days later. Each recruit row shows what the company adds to the daily wages and how many days of pay the purse holds after the hire; a hire that leaves under two days of wages asks first. In a town you can also refill your squads (fresh men water down a squad's experience) and train squads that have earned it to the next level, which makes their men tougher and deadlier and raises their wages. *Wait 8h* passes eight hours where you stand.
- **Places remember what happened.** Settlement vistas and map markers reflect current ownership, friendly or hostile banners, local services, garrison pressure, active raids, damage, rebuilding and night lighting. Towns stage markets and smiths, castles guards, and villages field work. The same saved facts produce the condition sentence, recent-event note and Vardish, Kharzani or Ryumon local ambience.
- **War and politics.** Realms track war weariness from casualties, raids, and losses. A realm whose home capital another realm holds (yours included) wants it back: at war with the holder, its marshal army goes for the capital before any other target ("to win back Khalun, its capital"), and at peace, once any truce has run out, it declares war for it while its weariness is 45 or less, with a good chance each day; a realm with no seat left to muster at can't. Exhausted realms seek peace treaties with 30-day truces and daily silver tributes. The Ryumon Shogunate maintains the mainland balance of power, going to war once if either rival holds 4 or more seats, and again only after any truce signed since runs out; a new peace between two realms replaces their old tribute. View active wars, truces, your standing with each realm and the weariness at which realms seek and accept peace on the War & Politics sheet.
- **Mercenary service.** Offer your sword to realms at war for a 30-day mercenary contract with daily pay (worked out each day from the men you bring) and lord capture bounties. Fighting under contract aligns your diplomacy with your employer; hostile realms bar you from their markets. A realm that bars you for your standing alone (−20 or below) can be sent an envoy with reparations from its Gates barred card: 100 silver and 10 more for every point below the line, once a week, buying your standing back to −15.
- **Armies, sieges, and conquest.** Marshals call expeditions of allied lords to march on enemy seats. Sieges construct rams and ladders, starve out defenders, resolve relief battles, and storm breached walls. Captured fortresses pass to the victor with all bound villages.
- **Allied battles and reserves.** Fights beyond warband scale feature a field cap of 12 active squads per side, with queued reserves marching in from the rear edge as frontline units break. Allied lord contingents fight under their own AI commanders with cyan trim and 1:1 casualty conservation.
- **Prisoners, ransom, and grudges.** Defeated enemy lords can be taken prisoner in your baggage. Settle ransom with envoys after 48 hours (paid from what their realm and purse hold, and the notice says when it falls short), deliver them to your employer, or release them for honor. A lord you beat rides home with only the men who got away, a captured lord's host scatters (half goes home), and each man lost wearies their realm. Enemy lords who slay your captains hold a grudge, hunt you on the road with custom dialogue, and fight with heightened morale until avenged.
- **The Chronicle and map readability.** Review the last 30 world and company events categorized by wars, sieges, battles, and deeds. Map hosts scale by troop strength, marshal armies group cleanly, bandits fade at wide zoom, fluttering heraldic banners indicate owner realms, and siege camps and burning ruins are drawn around settlements.
- **The Journal.** The map's Menu opens a journal of everything the company has promised: jobs, a contract, captive lords, your wedding (choosing a place, being made ready, ready, or postponed), gatherings you're invited to, your fief. Each shows its objective, reward, cost, time left and why it can't be done yet; *Show on map* finds it (and follows a hunted band as it moves), and one can be pinned to a card under the top bar. Recently ended promises say how they ended.
- **Playable sieges.** Under contract or holding a fief, ride to an enemy castle or town and *Storm the walls*: lead the assault in a battle with a wall across the field and a gate your men on foot must batter down (the garrison holds behind it, sheltered from arrows, until it gives), or send them in. Friendly lords camped at the walls join you. A win hands the place to your side, and some of the stormers (the lords beside you first, then your own men) stay on as its garrison. Riding with an allied army, you're asked to lead its assault; when an army storms your own fief while you're there, you can hold its walls yourself, with the garrison beside you. Besieged places are ringed on the map with the besiegers' camp, and their sheet shows both sides, the walls, the food left and when the assault comes.
- **Raiding.** At war with a realm, ride to one of its villages and *Raid the village* (from the map, or *Raid* on the town's Gates barred card). The side you serve is never fair game: under contract or sworn as a vassal, however low your standing with them falls, their villages can't be raided until the contract ends or you renounce your oath or raise your own crown. Its 15 militia scatter if you outnumber them three to one and nobody's stationed there; otherwise they fight for their homes, in a battle among the houses you can lead or send your men into. A burned village gives you 150 silver and 40 rations (and the spoils of any fight), pays its realm nothing and offers no recruits for a week, and adds to that realm's war weariness; the realm thinks worse of you (−10), and with a crown your Honorable lords think less of you (−3) and Cruel ones approve (+2). Your men then spend six hours loading the carts, and the realm's lords within reach ride to catch them, as they would any raider.
- **Your own crown.** The road is shown from the first day: *Toward a crown* on the Warband's Deeds tab (and on your seat's Fief tab) lists the five steps, each ticked or open with what it still needs: a fief, walls, 200 renown, no contract, and being at your seat. A famous company holding a castle or a town (not a village: a crown needs walls) can raise its own banner at its seat: the seat becomes the capital of a new realm in your colours, the old liege goes to war, and every castle or town you storm after that is yours, paying your purse and raising its garrison from the crown treasury; the seat keeps its guards, and a vassal granted a castle keeps that castle's. A vassal holding a village can ask their liege, from the village's Fief tab, for a castle that has lost its lord (standing kept; the village's buildings stay with it), and only squads you stationed can be taken back from a garrison. Its own screen (Menu → Your Crown) has four tabs:
  - *Realm*: name your crown and pick its colour (banners and the map follow), see the treasury and what each day brings in, draw from the treasury (your lords notice) or pay into it, and track the goal: hold Highcliff, Khalun and Kurogane and the Glass Sea is yours (+100 renown; play on as long as you like). Hold nothing for three days and the crown falls: its lords go back to their old allegiances, its wars end, and the company rides free to raise a banner again another day.
  - *Holdings*: every castle, town and village with its garrison, who holds it and a pin to find it. Grant a castle or town to a sworn lord (+15 Regard; whoever held it before resents losing it) and it becomes their seat; take it back at a cost. At a crown castle or town that isn't your seat, its Crown tab does the same and lets you station your own squads with the garrison. Stationed squads stay yours (through saves too) and draw half wages, from your seat's silver before your purse.
  - *Lords*: your sworn lords, their Regard, land and what they're doing. Order one to ride with you (they follow you and join your fights), to hold a place, or to go about the realm's business. The same orders are lines in a conversation with them.
  - *Court*: rule from anywhere. The tab is three cards, a gathering, the banners and petitions, each a line of status that opens a page of its own; what each choice is worth in Regard is on one rules sheet behind *?*, the war council puts each lord's face on the aim they back (tap a face to hear them), and *March!* shows who rides and what each lord makes of it before anyone moves. Your own hall reads the same way: the table's three actions (speech, gifts, gossip) as short buttons, a strip of faces to seat a lord at your right hand, petitions as a face, the ask and Grant or Refuse, the lists as your captains' faces to tap (up to three), and the result as who won, with every bout folded under it; *?* opens the same rules sheet. **Hold a gathering**, a feast, a dance or a **tourney** (300 silver, 200 rations, a 100-silver purse for a tourney; once every 20 days), at your seat or any castle or town of yours (a lord's hall means he hosts it in your name, +5 Regard); you needn't be there to call it, but miss the first night and the lords you were winning over notice (−3). At your own table, instead of a guest's toast and gift: a **seat of honour** for one lord (+8; lords of more renown feel passed over, −2), **gifts from the king** (30 silver a head, +3 each), **the king's speech** (renown 120; +2 with the hall, and at war it makes the case: +10 conviction with lords you're winning over, and your sworn lords count as ready to ride for a week), **petitions** heard at table (+5 rather than +2 from afar; a castle granted before everyone is +30 and +3 with every other lord who sees it), and at a tourney **the lists**: up to three of your captains against a champion of every lord in the hall, a single pass each bout, by the duel's own odds; the winner takes the purse (your captain: +10 renown and a bond with the others who rode; a lord's champion: +5 with him), every lord who rode +2, and a captain unhorsed is sometimes hurt, never killed. **Petitions** come from your lords' own state: a lord without land asks for the castle you have free (each castle to one lord at a time), a lord whose seat was burned asks for 100 silver from the treasury, a lord who thinks little of you wishes to be heard, and a band preying on a lord's lands is one petition (you take the hunt); settle or refuse (−5), and they don't ask again for a week. **Call the banners**: every sworn lord rides to a muster at a castle or town of yours (a red ring of spears on the map, "riding to the muster" on their plates, an entry in the journal), except those holding a place by your order, captives and the regrouping; a bought lord who thinks little of you refuses (−10). The treasury feeds the muster, a silver a day for every ten men; unfed for more than a day, it goes home. Once two lords are in the hall the **war council** sits (from the Court tab anywhere, or the hall itself): each lord speaks in his bearing's voice (Daring for the storm, Cautious to hold or relieve, Cruel to raid, Honorable for relief and against burning farms), you choose the aim (storm one of the three nearest enemy castles and towns; relieve a holding of yours under siege; hold the march between the muster and your holding nearest the enemy; raid the two nearest enemy villages) and who leads. **Led by you**, your lords ride at your side and join every fight and siege you take on; **led by a lord**, they march as a marshal's army, stand under a besieged holding until the siege breaks, ride the march back and forth, or burn the villages and come home. The treasury pays six days' provisions up front; if it can't, the host stays out three. Every lord who rides +5, once; a lord whose counsel you passed over −2; an Honorable lord asked to raid rides home (−5). The host goes home when its aim is met or its days are done, or when you dismiss it. The old quick call stays: two ready lords from your capital, on their own, against the castle you name.
  - *Diplomacy*: each realm: at war, at peace, allied or under truce, how weary of war they are, and what they pay or are paid. Declare war (your Honorable lords think less of a war you start, −5), propose an alliance, or sue for peace, whose terms show first: the wearier side pays the other tribute for a while. The crown's wars end only when you make peace, and a realm says when it would accept it now. A crown worth fearing draws rivals: a rested realm stronger than you may declare war on it.
  - Captive lords can swear fealty (honorable ones won't), and lords on the road can be won over in conversation.
- **Company deeds.** Eleven optional deeds, from serving a contract to its end to storming walls or keeping a captain alive through ten battles. The Warband shows your progress; each earned deed is a name the company can carry ("Known as the Wallbreakers"). Names bring nothing else, and no deed ends the campaign. The last, for a crown, is to hold every castle and town on the map at once (Keepers of the Glass Sea).
- **Camp feels like home.** The Warband's camp shows every captain at the fire, ready or resting with wounds, each a tap from their profile, under the light of the hour. One optional moment at a time comes from what really happened: a captain mourned by the friend they were bonded to, the captain who fought hardest speaking of the last battle and where it was fought, a shortage, a wound. Each has three lines for every temper, taken in turn, so the same one never comes twice running; a fallen captain waits to be mourned even if another battle comes first; and a hero speaks in their own voice.
- **Veteran promotions.** A captain with 5 battles or 15 kills earns a veteran role: Scout, Quartermaster or Drillmaster for the whole host (only one holder counts, and the screen says so), or Champion for their own company. The Warband flags it ("★ Promotion ready", and a count on Companies), and the captain's profile puts the choice first, each role with what it does and whom it reaches. A tap picks one and Promote makes it, since it can't be changed. The profile itself is cards under an arched portrait: the record as tiles, traits and role explained once, company progress and bonds as meters, each bond a tap from that captain, and Back pinned at the bottom.
- **Consequences that linger.** Notable battles are recorded where they happened, and for three days the map marks them with crossed blades, and changes of hands with a fresh banner, before they fade into the chronicle.
- **Saved as you go**, on the device (`user://campaign.json`): after every arrival, encounter and battle, and every few seconds on the road. Continue from the title screen. An autosave that can't be read is kept aside as `campaign.json.bad`, never written over, and Home says so and offers Load a save; one made by a newer version of the game is left untouched and not loaded. Starting a new campaign deletes your saves only after a "Start again?" sheet.
- **Saves of your own and going back**: up to 8 saves from the campaign menu, plus two the game keeps for you (just before your last battle, and when you last entered a settlement), in `user://saves/`. Revert, or tap any save, to see what going back would undo before it loads (better or worse marked ▲ and ▼). Saving over one of your own takes a second tap, as deleting does. Every save and its index are written whole and then renamed into place, and a damaged index is rebuilt from the save files; saves from a newer version show greyed. An **Ironman** campaign (chosen at the start) has only the autosave.

### The battles

- **Four troop types with a counter loop.** Spearmen stop cavalry, cavalry ride down archers, archers shred infantry, infantry cut through spear lines.
- **Squads of live soldiers.** Twenty men to a squad (sixteen horse), three ranks deep. Each man fights on his own, keeps formation on the march, and breaks ranks when the lines meet. Cavalry hit hard on the charge, except into spears.
- **Battles that take their time.** Two even squads grind at each other for the best part of a minute, and a full battle runs a minute or two, so there's time to see a fight turning and send help.
- **Archers with a quiver.** Eight arrows a man: deadly against troops marching at them, but they run dry, and then they're poor fighters. Arrows aimed into a melee with your own men mostly go wide. Bows sent to advance with nothing to loose (out of arrows, or holding their fire) don't walk into the melee with their knives: they wait behind the nearest company of their own foot and fight only whoever reaches them. Horse archers and skirmishing bowmen ride or run clear of foot that comes at them for only so long (about twelve seconds over a quiver); then they stand and shoot.
- **Morale and routing.** A squad that takes heavy losses breaks and runs for its own edge. Men who escape survive the battle.
- **Deploy your whole army.** Every squad in your warband is a card. Before the fight, place each one anywhere in your half of the field, with the enemy's line in plain view. Tap a placed squad to move it, or tap *Auto-place* for a battle line (swords and spears in front, bows behind, horse on the wings). Time stands still until you press *Fight*.
- **Two orders: Advance and Hold.** Squads hold their ground until ordered. *Advance* sends them at the enemy at marching pace, each going for the enemy it fights best, then charging when close. *Hold* stops them where they stand to fight whoever comes. The buttons command the whole army, or one squad once you select it (tap its card, its standard or its men); with a squad selected you can also tap an enemy squad to attack it (crossed swords and a pulsing ring mark every squad you've sent someone after, and the attackers stay on it until it breaks), or open ground to put a ring there and the ring again to march to it, so a stray tap never sends anyone. Squads on the march go around enemy squads in their way instead of through them, and one struck on the march stops there and fights back. Every order to go somewhere goes at marching pace until the last stretch, horse included, so a charge has the same run-up whether it came from *Advance*, a target or a march.
- **Carry a squad by its flag.** Press one of your standards and drag: the squad's men are ringed and a thread follows the finger. Over an enemy, their squad rings red and a card above the finger reads the matchup before you let go (▲ your men fight them best, ▼ they have the edge, · an even fight) with the numbers and their morale; let go to attack. Over open ground it reads *March here and hold*, or why not; letting go there marches it at once. Before the battle the same drag moves that one squad within your zone. A drag that starts off a flag still draws a line.
- **Hold to slow time.** An hourglass tab sits on the field's right edge in battle. Hold it with a thumb and the fight runs at a quarter pace, at 1× or 2× alike, under a cool cast with a *¼ time* badge, while the other hand taps or drags orders. Let go and it runs on. Pausing lets go of it. It only paces the screen: the battle plays out the same.
- **Draw a line.** Drag across the field and the whole army, or the squad you've picked, forms up along the line: each squad takes its own stretch in the order they already stand, so their paths don't cross, and faces away from the line toward the enemy. While you drag, the ground each squad will cover is outlined. A squad on its own widens or deepens its ranks to fill the line (never under two ranks). A line for the whole army waits on the field, its stretches outlined, until you tap it (a tap anywhere else drops it), so a stray swipe never re-forms the army. Before the battle, a line places the whole army at once, unless you have placed companies by hand: then it waits for the tap too.
- **Hold fire.** Archers can keep their arrows: they loose none, and draw blades if the enemy reaches them. A volley ends the hold. With no squad selected, *Hold fire (all bows)* and *Volley (all)* command every company of bows at once.
- **Braced lines.** A squad that has stood its ground on *Hold* for two seconds is braced: in the fight that reaches it, it hits a third harder and takes a quarter less from the front while the lines are locked (up to six seconds). Letting the enemy come is often the right call; *Advance* gives up the brace.
- **Lines that hold together (switched off).** Squads now fight by taking sides of an enemy (below), and this is off for now; the code stays behind `formation_melee` in `battle_sim.gd`. Switched on: when two lines of foot meet, each keeps its ranks: it turns slowly to face the squad it fights, closes to a front where the two lines meet and fights across it, every man staying near his place in the line, and when a front-rank man falls the man behind steps up. A squad on *Hold* meets the enemy where it stands, and squads side by side shuffle apart rather than merge, and where a longer line overlaps a shorter one its end men fold in on the flank. Horsemen, bowmen (light troops, even once their quivers are empty), men floundering in a ford, and anyone fighting them, still fight as a loose melee, and so do the walls of a siege.
- **Squads take a side, not a scrum.** A squad going for an enemy takes a free side of it: two foot squads to an enemy, the first at its front and the next on a flank, while horse ride in on any side still open, preferring a flank or the rear. A third foot squad goes for another enemy, or, with none to spare, waits behind a friend's line as a reserve, stepping in when the enemy reaches it. A squad heading for a flank goes round the corner and joins the fight there, not on its way past the front, and the side it chose stays put on the ground, so an enemy that turns doesn't lead it in circles. An order to attack a particular enemy goes in whatever; archers shooting, ambushes and siege assaults go as before.
- **Flanks and rear.** A squad locked in one fight can't turn to meet a second enemy. Blows from beside or behind it land harder and shake its nerve, and horse that take spearmen from behind land their charge. Pin them with foot, then send in the horse.
- **One ability per squad.** Infantry raise a *Shield wall* (arrows and blows from the front barely land, but they move at half pace). Spearmen form a *Schiltron* (a ring of spears with no flank or rear, and no charge lands). Archers loose a *Volley* (every bow at once, harder, and it shakes the target's nerve). Cavalry *Wheel* (break off, ride clear, charge the same enemy again). Each has a cooldown (shorter or longer for some trained branches), which drains on its button.
- **Win by breaking their army.** The battle ends when one side has no squad left standing. After five minutes, the side with the larger share of its army still fighting (its reserve still to come counts) wins: the strength bar at the top draws each side's share, with a notch in the middle and "+N in reserve" beside a side whose reserve has yet to march on, so the bar says who the clock would favour.
- **Retreat has a price.** From the pause card you can pull your army off the field, and the button says what it costs: free to go, each company still fighting leaves a share of its men behind as it breaks off (more from those locked in a fight), the number on the button. When the map said there was no way out (an ambush, or a lord's riders across the road), Retreat is a run for it: every company breaks for your edge, the battle goes on, and the enemy cuts down whoever it catches. Before the fight you can still ride away free, unless you were cut off: that is the map's Withdraw, not a lost battle. A retreat once the lines have met counts as a defeat, and you ride home as from any lost field.
- **Enemy commanders with a temperament**, which your scouts report while you deploy. *Reckless* comes straight at you. *Patient* holds a braced line, takes your charge, then sends in the rest. *Hammer* pins you with foot and holds its horse until the lines meet, then rides for your archers and flanks. All of them use their squads' abilities, and all but the reckless use the ground: they shift their whole line onto the best footing in reach (the crest of a rise, the edge of a wood, the near bank of a ford), put their archers on the heights, and keep that ground for a while rather than wade a river or leave a ridge to come at you. A chief's bodyguard stands behind his line and rides out only to finish a fight or meet a threat.
- **A battle you can read on a phone.** The camera follows the fighting. Every squad carries a standard showing its type, morale and men, swallowtails for you and square flags for them; a squad's standard topples when it breaks. Select a squad and the enemy standards show which ones it beats and which beat it. A tray of squad cards along the bottom shows every squad's morale and whether its ability is ready; every morale meter carries its shape too (▲ steady, ◆ shaken, ▼ near breaking), and a tapped enemy shows its morale as a number, so none of it rests on colour alone. Every order button keeps its word under its icon. A strength bar replaces the clock. Pause any time (orders still work while paused: select, target and draw lines on the field as well as the buttons), play at double speed, and the battle pauses itself if you leave the app; Escape or the phone's back button pauses and resumes too. Pinch or scroll to zoom and drag with two fingers (or the right mouse button) to look around: the camera then holds where you put it until *Re-center*.
- **Pixel-art troops, dressed by culture and by training.** Original top-down sprites for each type in both team colors, different for each people: Vardish footmen with kite shields and steel helms, kettle-hatted spearmen, hooded bowmen and horsemen in steel caps; Kharzani askari in white turbans with round shields and scimitars, spearmen, recurve bowmen and faris on grey horses; Ryumon samurai in black lacquer with crested helmets and sashimono banners at their backs, ashigaru under wide straw hats, long-bowed yumi archers and riders on dark horses. Each of the 24 trained branches wears its own kit, on the field and in its portrait: men-at-arms in plate with great helms, sergeants with maces and plumes, pikemen, halberdiers with poleaxes, crossbowmen with pavises on their backs, longbowmen, barded knights; javelin askari, gilded guard askari, spear guard in mail, shieldless levy spears, veiled desert archers, horse archers and faris lancers with pennons; hatamoto under horned crests, ronin with no banner, yari ashigaru and ashigaru archers under black lacquered hats, naginata, yumi samurai with a katana at the hip, mounted samurai with the horo billowing behind them, and mounted archers. The upgrade choice shows both. A squad you hire abroad wears its own culture's gear. Two walk frames and a strike pose each, a white flash when hit, shadows, fallen bodies, trampled and bloodied ground where they fell, and arrows that arc in flight and stick in the turf where they miss. Charges shake the screen and land with a jolt.
- **Fields by land.** A battle is fought on the ground of the realm it's in: Vardish meadow, Kharzani steppe with pebbles and scrub, Ryumon moss with fallen petals, with rocks, bushes and patches of lighter and darker ground.
- **A battlefield that shows pressure.** A squad close to breaking is marked wavering before it runs, its standard trembling; a squad that halts settles into its ground; arrows at the edge of the field point to your selected company, or to any of yours fighting out of sight. Big moments cut through the noise of routine fighting, charges throw up spray, leaf litter, sand or dust by the ground, and an optional vibration answers accepted orders and decisive moments.
- **Siege battles.** A wall crosses the field with a gate in it. Men on foot at the gate batter it down (horse at half the pace; horses can't climb the ladders). Rout the garrison to win; a gate still standing when time runs out means the walls held. Defend your own walls the same way from the other side.
- **A wall with a top and a hole.** While the gate holds it's a door: nobody fights through it, and the garrison never steps outside its own wall. The fighting happens where a wall is weak. The garrison has posts: bows on the wall walk beside each ladder (the battlements there halve arrows; the courtyard behind gets no cover), a company of swords on the gate tower dropping stones on the men at the ram (whose roof takes most of it), and the rest of its foot in a crescent behind the gate where the breach will come. When men go up a ladder, the nearest free company on foot climbs to the ladder's head; a held head slows the climb, and the man topping the ladder fights one-handed. When the gate breaks, the garrison's idle foot falls back to the crescent and holds the breach, and it sallies only once the fight turns its way (or nobody has come for it in a while). The attackers spread out too: *Storm* (and an enemy storming your walls) sends the companies furthest out on each side up a ladder each when there are three or more on foot, the rest to the ram, and the bows stand off at bow range to shoot the walk rather than walk up to the wall; once their quivers are empty they wait for the gate. Holding your own walls, *Auto-place* puts your companies at their posts (drawn while you deploy: the gate tower, the ladder heads, the crescent), *Man the walls* sends everyone back to them, *Hold breach* forms the crescent once the gate is down, and *Advance all* becomes *Sally*. In seeded AI battles on a castle, an even assault took it in none of eight (the garrison lost about a quarter of its men), half again the garrison won 20 of 32 and lost some 84 men to the garrison's 58 on average, and twice the garrison won all eight. While you deploy, the ground the wall's bows reach is shaded, along with the cover under the ram's roof and the two ladders. *Storm* shows its plan first (dashed routes from each company to a ladder or the ram, and the battle waits); tap a card to swap a company between ladder and ram, then *Storm!* to go. Each company's card says what it's doing (climbing, on the walls, at the ram, inside, and for a garrison: stones on the tower, holding a ladder, at the breach), the gate has its own bar over the gatehouse, the breach gets a banner, and the result tells the siege's story: who held the castle after, the blows on the gate, the men who went over the ladders, the stones dropped and the men thrown off the ladders, and when each turn came.
- **Captains who can die.** Every squad is led by a named captain ("Brann's Shields"), a tougher man at the heart of the formation marked by a gold ring, with a temperament (Vengeful, Steadfast, Fragile) and sometimes a quirk (Lucky, Mentor, Bold). The squad holds its nerve better under them and reels when they go down.
- **The death roll.** A fallen captain rolls a d100 against a death chance of 45%, less 15 each if their own squad still has half its men, another squad is close enough to drag them clear, or they're Lucky. Over the chance: wounded, and they sit out the next two battles. At or under: dead. The battle all but stops while the die rolls on screen against the odds, with each modifier shown, then their fate and last words (some in their own people's way); the dead go on the memorial in camp while a veteran takes over the company.
- **Bonds.** Captains who fight side by side build a bond, rank C, B then A. Bonded pairs fighting near each other regain nerve and, from B, hit harder. When one falls, the other reacts by temperament: fury, a steadied line, or a squad that starts to break. The Warband's Bonds tab shows talks waiting at the fire first (its tab, the camp and the map's Warband button count them), then bonds closest to their next rank, pairs still growing toward C, and the remembered. A talk at the fire puts the two captains face to face by the campfire at night: what the company remembers of their battles, then a short exchange in their own words, by both their tempers (a hero's in their own). The remembered open too: the survivor's talk by the empty place, the fallen face greyed. Their talks go in order, rank C, then B, then A, none skipped: a pair that climbed two ranks between visits hears both, one after the other, and once all are heard the latest is told again.
- **Captains' faces.** Every captain has one face, drawn in pixel art from who they are and kept on every screen: a jaw and cheekbones lit from the upper left, eyes with an iris and a lid, hair that hangs behind the head, and clothes of their people. Their life shows on it: scars for each wound, grey with age and long service, a bandage while they recover, brass at the collar when seasoned and a short cloak at twenty battles, the troops they lead (a quiver, a cloak clasp, a spear's tassel, a slung shield), and a token of a veteran role (a Scout's feather, a Quartermaster's keys, a Drillmaster's whistle, a Champion's laurel). Their temper sets their resting face, and the moment changes it: in pain on the death roll, grieving for a fallen friend, warm at the fire, proud just promoted.
- **A persistent warband** with its bonds and the memorial of the fallen, on the Warband screen from the map.

### Sound and feel

- **Music for every place**, composed for the game (`tools/music.py`, `tools/orchestral.py`). Home opens on The Glass Sea, played by a recorded orchestra: harp alone, then the horn's tune, then the violins take it up over the whole orchestra before it settles; once you have a campaign, Home plays your own realm's tune by the campfire instead. On the map, the music of the realm you're riding through, crossfading as you cross a border: lute and recorder over a hurdy-gurdy drone in Vardmark, oud and ney over a frame drum's rhythm in Kharzan, koto and shakuhachi with taiko on the Ryumon isles.
- **Battle music in layers**: while you deploy, a drone, a heartbeat drum and a call to arms in the enemy's style (Vardmark's horns, Kharzan's ney, Ryumon's shakuhachi and taiko; sieges have their own, heavier). Press Fight and the full music starts: drums and a string ostinato written to carry on a phone speaker, the tune swelling in as more of the field is fighting, bright trumpets when the fight goes your way and dark tremolo when your lines waver. Each piece runs a minute, in four sections with a breakdown before the climax. The victory or defeat call waits for the beat, then a quiet piece plays under the results.
- **Battle effects**: sparks fly where blows land, with a spatter now and then, and a warm glow along the front shows where the lines meet. Dust trails every squad on the move, thicker behind horses and coloured by the ground, and charging riders stream speed lines. Arrows trail streaks and the ground darkens where a volley will land. Men stagger and fall rather than vanish, leaving shield, weapon and a dark patch; a broken squad strews its gear and leaves its standard in the mud. Standards ripple in the wind; a wavering squad's men glance back over their shoulders; when their captain falls, time slows and the field darkens round him; and when the battle is won, the winners cheer under glowing standards before the result comes up. Reduced motion stills the ripple, glances, cheer and speed lines.
- **Drawing a battle cheaply**: every man in a battle is one draw. Each frame of each kind of soldier, in each side's colours, sits in a single atlas texture (`scripts/art/unit_art.gd`), and the men and their shadows go into a MultiMesh on their own layer, with a small shader that picks each man's frame and paints his hit flash; the dead are one draw per kind of body. The field is three layers, bottom to top: the ground, the dead and the orders; the men; then trees, arrows in flight, standards and words. On the same frozen 12-against-12 battle the drawing code went from about 19 ms a frame to 13, and the renderer's own work from 34 ms to 24 (measured with a software renderer; a phone's GPU takes most of the second number off the CPU). **Settings › Show performance** puts a readout at the bottom left of the field: what the sim, the drawing and the renderer cost a frame, frames a second, the worst frame of the last second, and how many men and companies are on the field. Under the men, the ground, its slopes and props are drawn once into layers that the camera only moves, and what lies on the ground (scars, dropped gear, arrows stuck in the earth) is redrawn at most twice a second, the newest drawn each frame until then. Each standard's cloth and glyph is built once as one triangle list and waved by a shader, so a flag is one draw. A squad choosing a target checks only the squads already going for each enemy. Together these took the standards from 1.9 ms a frame to 0.7, the field pass from 2.5 ms to about 1.3, and target picking in the sim from 1.5 ms a tick to 0.5. Keeping the crowd apart reads every man's place and size once into flat arrays rather than looking them up for each pair, which makes the whole sim about a fifth faster and helps most where men are packed tightest. The battles come out the same move for move.
- **Men who fight, not flash.** Each man draws back before a blow, lunges into it on the strike frame and eases back to his place; a hit knocks him back and turns him; a charge shoves the men it strikes back the way the horses came, the rank behind half as far, and men duck under a volley coming down. The dying stagger before they fall, with dust where they land. A braced line, a shield wall or a schiltron crouches into the blow; men waiting breathe on their own beat; routers face the edge they run for and lean into it. Where the lines meet, men are drawn back to front across squads. All of it is drawn only: the same seeded battle ends the same with the screen drawing it or not (`tests/battle_feel_tests.gd`).
- **Impact, and words with a weight.** A charge on or by your men stops the battle for longer the more men it strikes (charges landing together share one stop), knocks the view the way it went and punches in toward it. Callouts come in three tiers: a moment (your squad breaking, a captain falling, the chief or a lord falling, the gate) stamps onto the field on a plate, one at a time, while the rest wait; a squad's word (CHARGE, BRACED, FLANK, a broken enemy) is a pill over its standard, one per squad and never twice in three seconds; a state (wavering, out of arrows) is a badge on the standard and the card, with no words.
- **The hour of the fight.** A battle is fought at the hour the world's clock says: dawn is pale and warm with mist on the far edge and long shadows east, dusk orange with long shadows west, night blue with a vignette and light pooled round the standards. One wind per battle carries the dust.
- **A start and an end.** *Fight* puts up a title card (the place, the ground and the hour, the odds and the scouts' word on their commander) while the view pulls out from your line; the battle waits for it, and a tap clears it. When it's decided, VICTORY stamps on the field under a spotlight on the last squad to break, or the colour drains from the field under DEFEAT, before the result, which now opens on a picture of the field. The tray feels the fight: a card's edge flashes red when its squad is charged or flanked, a broken squad's card cracks and shakes, morale bars and the strength bar ease down with a pale bite for what was just lost, and an ability coming ready pulses once with a click. Pausing greys the field. The death roll's plate drops in, its die tumbles and bounces as it lands, the odds fill before the number, and FALLEN stamps down. *Screen shake: off, light or full* in Settings; reduced motion keeps every word and state and stills every offset, stamp, zoom and shake, the hit flash on each man, the gate's flash and the stop when a charge lands. An enemy lord's fall is one roll: the moment says he falls, and whether he is taken or slain, as the result will. The one-time card about the ground or the objective comes up once *Fight* is pressed, and the battle waits for it. Wounded men are explained on the result: they rejoin over the coming days, and still eat and draw pay.
- **Battle sounds** that play where they happen, from the left or right of the screen, duller and quieter when you're zoomed out. Blows ring out where the lines meet, matched to who's fighting (sword on sword, a thunk on a shield, spear shafts knocking, axes and maces crashing), each in a dozen takes that never repeat back to back, over the din of the melee that swells and fades with the fighting. Arrows, bolts and javelins are heard going up and coming down. Men roar as they charge, horsemen thunder into the line, shields lock with a shout when spears brace, a squad that breaks scatters shouting while the other side cheers, a captain falls with a gasp from the ranks. Sieges have the ram's blows on the gate, ladders clattering on stone and the gate giving way. Each realm sounds its own calls to start, charge and retreat (Vardmark's horns and trumpets, Kharzan's shawm and drums, Ryumon's conch and taiko), and its distant horn when more of them come. Built from recorded metal and wood (public domain) with synthesized bodies and crowds, every sound carries on a phone speaker, and a limiter keeps the loudest moments clean. Each battle sound plays at its own target level over the battle music (which sits lower than music elsewhere): routine blows and arrows a few dB over the drums, charges, horns and a captain's fall well over them, the din of the melee under them. Every take is trimmed from its measured level (`tools/sfx_levels.py` writes `scripts/data/sfx_levels.gd`), so no blow is lost to a quiet recording.
- **Other sounds**: the snap and twang of crossbows, the whoosh of thrown javelins, horse archers loosing on the move, a fanfare when a chief's guard breaks or he falls, the river running through a ford and birds in a wood under the battle music, the rattle of the death roll's die and a bell when it lands on death; on the map, open-road wind, woods, water, night and settlement-edge ambience crossfade under foot or cavalry movement, with an alarm horn when bandits block the road and coins when you buy or hire.
- **Feel**: screens fade into one another (a plain cut under Reduced motion), every button clicks and gives under your finger, map sheets and the battle result ease into view, and the title screen drifts over the Glass Sea at dawn. Music and sound can each be switched off on the dedicated Settings page, alongside text size, reduced motion, screen shake and vibration (and full screen on a computer); the choices are kept on the device. A tap anywhere on a setting's row flips its switch. Escape, or a phone's back, closes the sheet on top or goes back a page, and on the map opens or closes the campaign menu.
- **Polish**: men, standards and arrows are drawn between the sim's 30 steps a second, so a battle moves at the display's rate and the slow paces glide rather than jump. Steel rings when and where a blow lands. Each order has its own short cue (a tick to take a squad in hand, a drum to march, a beat to arms to attack) and a refused one knocks and is felt. Slowed or paused, the field is heard through a wall and the music steps back. Battles open on wind over open ground or the night's quiet. Vibrations come in five weights. On the map a flick carries on, the edges give, Show on map glides, the view follows the party while it rides until you pan away, and a settlement's sheet draws the road there. The road autosave is written on a worker thread. Into battle, a shutter closes and opens on the field. Text is set in Bannerfall Text, Alegreya Sans drawn larger on its em with tabular figures (`tools/body_font.py`). Settings has music and sound volume sliders, and under Developer, multipliers for shake, hit-stop, men's motion, camera follow and vibration (`tests/feel_polish_tests.gd`; see `docs/feel-polish-plan.md`).

## Run it

1. Download **Godot 4.7** (the standard build, not .NET) from [godotengine.org](https://godotengine.org/download).
2. Open Godot, choose **Import**, and select `project.godot` in this folder.
3. Press **Play** (F5). The window is phone-shaped; a mouse click works as a tap. You start at the title screen: New campaign, then choose your people.

## Put it on your iPhone

You need a Mac with Xcode and an Apple ID (a free one works for your own phone).

1. In Godot: **Editor → Manage Export Templates → Download and Install**.
2. **Project → Export → iOS** (the preset is already there). Fill in **App Store Team ID** (your team ID from Xcode or developer.apple.com; it is left empty in the repository) and, if it isn't yours, change the **Bundle Identifier** (for example `com.yourname.bannerfall`).
3. **Export Project** to a folder. Godot writes an Xcode project.
4. Open the `.xcodeproj` in Xcode, plug in your iPhone, pick it as the run destination, and press **Run**.

The project uses the Compatibility renderer, which also runs in the iOS Simulator. Godot's guide: [Exporting for iOS](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/export/exporting_for_ios.html).

## Other platforms

`export_presets.cfg` also has **Android** (arm64, portrait, the vibrate permission for haptics), **Windows Desktop**, **macOS** and **Linux** presets, all at version 0.1.0. The Android package name `com.bannerfall.game` is a placeholder: confirm it before the first Play Console upload, since a published package name can never change (a Play Store upload also wants a Gradle build exported as an AAB). The app icons are `assets/icon_1024.png` (opaque, on the splash colour), `icon_512`, `icon_192`, `icon_180`, `icon.ico` and the Android adaptive layers, all rendered from `icon.svg`. On a computer F11 or Alt+Enter toggles full screen, and menus keep to a phone-width column in a wide window.

**Moving a campaign.** Everything lives in Godot's user folder: `campaign.json` (the autosave), `saves/` (your own and the kept saves, with `saves/index.json`) and `settings.cfg`. On iPhone and iPad the folder shows in the Files app (On My iPhone › Bannerfall); on a computer the Saves screen says where it is. Copy those to the same place on the other device. For Steam, Steam Cloud needs no code: in Steamworks (Cloud › Auto-Cloud) add the user folder's `campaign.json` and `saves/*.json` for each OS.

## How it's built

| Path | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `scenes/title.tscn` | Home: your company's card and what's waiting, Continue; or the three peoples to begin with. New campaign, tutorial and Settings. |
| `scenes/settings.tscn`, `scenes/tutorial.tscn` | Device audio preferences and an optional six-step walkthrough. |
| `scenes/bond_intro.tscn` | The saved first-rank-C bond introduction. |
| `scenes/new_campaign.tscn` | Choose your people. |
| `scenes/world_map.tscn` | The map of the Glass Sea: travel, settlements, encounters. |
| `scenes/settlement.tscn` | A settlement: buy food, hire recruits, reinforce and train. |
| `scenes/main_menu.tscn` | The Warband screen: purse, squads, captains, bonds, the fallen, reinforcing. |
| `scenes/battle.tscn` | The battle screen. |
| `scripts/data/troops.gd` | Troop stats, the counter bonuses, abilities and veterancy. **Tune the game here.** |
| `scripts/data/units.gd` | Culture unit upgrade trees (24 tier-2 branch options), mechanics (armor, piercing, javelins, horse archers, sweep, steady), stats and wages. |
| `scripts/data/terrain_rules.gd` | Movement and combat modifiers across height, woods, water, and obstacles. |
| `scripts/battle/terrain.gd` | Battlefield ground representations: elevations, forests, rivers, shallow fords, and rocks. |
| `scripts/battle/battle_site.gd` | Battlefield site generation based on world map location, biome, and encounter objective. |
| `scripts/battle/pathing.gd` | Grid A* formation pathfinding navigating around rocks, deep water, and across fords. |
| `scripts/battle/battle_sim.gd` | Battle rules as pure logic: deployment, orders, movement, combat, morale, objectives, reinforcements, win conditions. No drawing. |
| `scripts/battle/commander_ai.gd` | The computer's commanders: how each temperament lines up, when it commits, uses abilities, handles terrain, and reacts to objectives. |
| `scripts/battle/auto_resolve.gd` | Settles a fight without playing it, from each side's strength. Fitted to the battle sim; the tests check they still agree. |
| `scripts/battle/battle_screen.gd` | Drawing, touch input and the HUD. Reads the sim; never changes the rules. |
| `scripts/art/unit_art.gd` | Builds the sprite textures, unit portraits and battlefield ground at startup, by culture. |
| `scripts/art/sprite_data.gd` | The unit pixel art itself, as rows of palette letters. Generated: don't edit by hand. |
| `tools/unit_sprites.py` | Where the unit pixel art is drawn, culture by culture, with the battlefield's rocks and bushes. Edit this, then run `python3 tools/unit_sprites.py preview/` to regenerate `sprite_data.gd` and a preview sheet. |
| `scripts/art/map_art.gd`, `scripts/art/map_sprite_data.gd` | The same for the map: settlements by culture and type, terrain, and parties. |
| `tools/map_sprites.py` | Where the map's pixel art is drawn. `python3 tools/map_sprites.py preview/`. |
| `shaders/` | The map's sea, land and sky (the hour's light and cloud shadows). |
| `tools/music.py` | Composes and synthesizes the map themes and all the sound effects with numpy, and encodes them to Ogg Vorbis with ffmpeg into `assets/audio/`. `python3 tools/music.py` renders everything, or name one piece, `sfx`, or a prefix such as `'base_*'`. |
| `tools/battle_sfx.py` | The battle sounds: clash banks, the melee's din, missiles landing, crowds, siege sounds and each realm's calls, layered from recorded metal and wood (the same CC0 library) and synthesis. `python3 tools/music.py battle_sfx` renders them. |
| `tools/orchestral.py`, `tools/sampler.py` | The orchestral pieces (Home, the campfire versions, the battle's layers, its stingers and aftermath), played by recorded instruments from the VS Chamber Orchestra Community Edition (public domain, CC0). Run `tools/fetch_samples.sh` once to fetch the few folders used (not committed; the rendered music is). |
| `tools/body_font.py` | Builds `assets/fonts/BannerfallText.ttf`, the body face, from Alegreya Sans Medium: drawn 12% larger on its em (Open Sans's x-height and line height at a given size, a tenth narrower), digits tabular and lining, hinting dropped, renamed as the OFL asks. Needs fontTools. |
| `scripts/audio/sound.gd` | Music and sound (an autoload): music and ambience crossfades, separate movement loops, the battle's music (deployment, then four layers in step faded by the fighting and by who is winning, a stinger on the beat and a piece under the results), an effects pool and a pool of placed battle sounds (takes that never repeat, groups for melee, missiles, voices and cues through a limiter, caps on how many play at once, the field dulled when the view is far off, the melee's din following the fight), button clicks, and the music and sound switches. |
| `scripts/ui/scenes.gd` | Changes screens with a quick fade (an autoload). |
| `scripts/data/captains.gd` | Captain traits, the death roll and bond ranks. Tune the odds here. |
| `scripts/data/lords.gd` | The lords: names and titles by people, bearings, warband sizes, treasuries and dues, and how far they ride for each job. **Tune the lords here.** |
| `scripts/data/economy.gd` | Prices, wages, food, desertion, spoils, renown and the squad cap. **Tune the campaign's pace here.** |
| `scripts/data/cultures.gd` | The three cultures: names, colors, captain name pools, and what each calls its troops. |
| `scripts/world/world_data.gd` | The map as data: factions, settlements, roads, sea lanes, rivers and their fords, coastlines (with their ragged shores) and bandit bands, and who holds what and who is at war when a campaign starts. |
| `scripts/world/world_sim.gd` | The world as pure logic: the clock, travel by road, who holds each settlement and which realms are at war, the lords and what they do, bandits, encounters, saving. No drawing. |
| `scripts/world/map_screen.gd` | Draws the map's settlements, parties, light and names, and turns taps into travel. Reads the world sim; never changes the rules. |
| `scripts/world/travel_presentation.gd` | Derives terrain, time, culture, movement contact and factual arrival presentation from the world without changing it or consuming campaign RNG. |
| `scripts/world/map_backdrop.gd` | The sea and the land under the map and the title screen. |
| `scripts/world/map_terrain.gd` | Works out the map's look once: the land picture its shaders read, and where woods, peaks, dunes and fields stand. |
| `scripts/world/settlement_screen.gd` | The settlement screen. Shows prices and passes taps to the campaign; never changes the rules. |
| `scripts/ui/heraldry.gd` | Flags and troop glyphs, shared by the battle's standards and the map's pennants. |
| `scripts/game_state.gd` | The campaign (an autoload): your culture, the warband, its purse, food and renown, hiring and training, daily upkeep, battle results and the save file. |
| `scripts/save_slots.gd` | Saves beyond the autosave: your own (up to 8), before battle and on arrival, each with a summary so the list and the "what going back undoes" sheet never load a campaign. |
| `scripts/ui/style.gd` | Shared colors and UI theme. |
| `tests/run_tests.gd` | Headless checks of the rules. |
| `tests/plans.gd` | Scripted players for the balance check, and a scripted campaign for the economy check. |

The rules don't touch the screen, in battles and on the map alike, so both sims can be tested headless. Battles take and return plain data: an army goes in as `[{id, type, men, xp, level}]`, and `result_for()` hands back survivors and kills per squad. Auto-resolve returns the same shape, so a fight you send your men into lands on the warband exactly like one you led.

## Tests

For the feel-and-polish pass (camera, autosave, battle interpolation, cues, sound, settings), run `godot --headless --path . --script res://tests/feel_polish_tests.gd`.

For menu navigation, tutorial opt-in and replay, first-bond save behavior, resource controls and desertion warnings, run `godot --headless --path . --script res://tests/menu_tests.gd`. It uses a temporary preferences file and does not save its test campaign.

From the project folder:

```
godot --headless --script res://tests/run_tests.gd
```

On a Mac, `godot` is the binary inside the app: `/Applications/Godot.app/Contents/MacOS/Godot`.

To smoke-test the whole loop, run the game with `-- --autoplay`: it starts a Vardish campaign, rides at the nearest bandits, lets the computer fight both sides of the battle, returns to the map, sends the men in against the next band, then enters the next settlement it reaches, buys food, hires and reinforces, and quits.

It checks the deployment rules (no placing in the enemy half, squads dropped on each other get nudged apart, Auto-place fits everyone, the clock waits for *Fight*), that *Hold* keeps a squad in place and *Advance* sends it forward, that a squad struck on the march stops and fights back, that horse sent at a target ride at marching pace until the last stretch, that a retreat costs men (and, cut off, is a run for it the enemy can ride down), that the strength verdict counts a reserve still to come, that archers run out of arrows and then wait behind their own foot rather than walk into the melee, that skirmishers ride clear for only so long, lines (squads share a drawn line in the order they stand, without overlaps, centered where it was drawn; they march there, hold and face the enemy; one squad alone widens or deepens to fill it; before the battle a line places everyone where the preview showed, and stays in your half), hold fire (archers keep every arrow and fight with blades; a volley ends the hold), the tactics (only a pinned squad can be flanked; horse in the rear of a pinned squad break it sooner, at no greater cost; a braced line wins the even fight it receives; horse sent at archers ride around the spears in front of them), each ability, that each kind of enemy commander behaves as its scouts say, the cultures (each warband's captains and companies come from its own culture, and it starts at its capital), the world (every settlement reaches every other by road, settlements and roads lie on land and sea lanes cross water, travel takes exactly the time the map says, the clock stands still when you do, a band on the road stops you and the clock, a beaten band comes back a day later in its own realm, a saved world loads back exactly and carries on exactly as the original, a castle that changes hands takes its villages along and a realm that loses its capital rules from what it has left, owners and wars are saved, a save from before they could change still loads, sixty days of riding never puts more than two bands in one realm, and battle results reach the map), the economy (a day costs a day's wages and a ration a man; hungry or unpaid men desert in proportion to the shortfall; hiring brings a full squad of the offering place's culture, only where you stand and only under the squad cap, which renown raises; offers come back after three days; reinforcing costs silver and waters down experience; training raises level and wages; spoils and renown are paid; a band that beats you keeps only its survivors), auto-resolve (the side it reckons stronger wins the full battle at least 85% of the time over sixteen random battles, with about the same losses; every man is accounted for; men sent in without you lose more; captains can fall), six scripted 30-day campaigns that settle every fight unled (`Plans.campaign`: every warband lasts the month without dropping below 50 men, rarely deserts or goes broke, makes a name, and most grow), that each counter wins its duel at least 80% of the time, that mirror matches are close to even, that full AI-vs-AI battles finish with an army breaking and last about a minute, and that every man is accounted for in the battle result. It takes a few minutes and exits with code 1 if anything fails.

Add `-- --balance` to also fight three scripted players against every kind of commander, twelve battles each (`tests/plans.gd`): one who presses *Advance all* and nothing else, one who holds all battle, and a tactician who receives the charge braced, uses volleys and shield walls, and sends the horse into flanks. It checks that skill pays: *Advance all* wins about half the time at most, the tactician at least 70%. It also checks the Phase 3 gates: mirrored maps of every kind are fair both ways round; attacking a band that holds a ridge, a ford or a wood's edge with 15% more men wins at least 15 points less often than on open ground; each culture's trained army is a match for each other's; the tactician wins each objective at least 60% of the time, and clearly more often than *Advance all*; and neither branch of a role outclasses the other. It takes the best part of an hour.
